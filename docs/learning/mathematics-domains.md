# Mathematics domain coverage

MathKX aims to cover mathematics from foundational arithmetic through university-level study. This coverage map gives contributors a shared view of the domains and topics we intend to support, while keeping the first implementation focused on a small set of useful skills and exercises.

The university scope initially targets broad undergraduate mathematics, including subjects used in mathematics, science, engineering, computing, and statistics courses. Specialist graduate and research topics can be considered later. The topics below are a coverage goal, not existing content, a complete degree syllabus, or a release schedule.

## How content is organized

Learners can browse **domains → topics → skills → exercise families**. Domains and topics organize discovery; the prerequisite graph connects the actual skills, including connections across domains. A skill may appear in several browsing paths without acquiring a second identity or a separate progress record.

School grades, curricula, and university courses map onto these reusable skills. A course called Calculus I or a grade-specific mathematics course supplies a learning path, not a new copy of its skills. Domain names below are human-readable groupings; they do not establish permanent identifier prefixes, directories, or content schemas.

Progression should use explicit capabilities rather than a universal sequence of numbered levels. Within a skill, exercise features such as number types, reasoning demands, representations, and available support describe variations in challenge. Mastery remains an estimate of a skill, not completion of a domain or course.

## Foundational and school mathematics

These domains establish knowledge that remains useful throughout university study. Their placement here identifies common entry points, not an age restriction or a ceiling on depth.

| Domain | Intended topics | Connections to further study |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Numbers and arithmetic | Counting, quantity, place value, comparison, the four operations, negative numbers, fractions, decimals, percentages, ratios, proportional reasoning, powers, roots, estimation, rounding, and scientific notation | Numerical fluency supports algebra, measurement, probability, and computation; number systems connect to number theory and algebraic structures. |
| Measurement and units | Length, mass, time, angles, area, volume, unit conversion, scales, rates, precision, and dimensional reasoning | Supports geometry, physical modeling, calculus applications, and interpretation of numerical results. |
| Algebra and equations | Patterns, variables, expressions, equality, substitution, rearrangement, expansion, factorization, linear and quadratic equations, inequalities, simultaneous equations, polynomials, rational expressions, radicals, and complex-number arithmetic | Connects to functions, linear algebra, calculus, and abstract algebra. |
| Functions and graphs | Coordinates, relations, function notation, domain and range, tables and graphs, linear and quadratic functions, polynomial and rational functions, exponential and logarithmic functions, transformations, composition, inverses, sequences, and recursive definitions | Connects representations and modeling to calculus, analysis, discrete mathematics, and differential equations. |
| Geometry | Shapes and their properties, spatial reasoning, constructions, congruence, similarity, transformations, coordinates, distances, circles, solid geometry, geometric argument, and the Pythagorean theorem | Connects to trigonometry, vectors, analytic geometry, proof, and later geometry and topology. |
| Trigonometry | Right-triangle ratios, radians, the unit circle, trigonometric functions and graphs, identities, equations, sine and cosine rules, and periodic models | Supports calculus, vectors, complex numbers, oscillation, and differential equations. |
| Probability | Chance, sample spaces, events, counting outcomes, relative frequency, conditional probability, independence, Bayes' theorem, random variables, common discrete and continuous distributions, expectation, variance, joint distributions, and introductory limit theorems | Connects elementary chance to university probability, statistical inference, and stochastic modeling. |
| Statistics and data | Data collection, sampling, bias, tables and charts, measures of center and spread, association, regression, study design, estimation, confidence intervals, hypothesis testing, likelihood, and introductory Bayesian inference | Connects data literacy to university statistics and applied investigations; probability and computation support later topics. |

## Foundations for reasoning and discrete mathematics

Reasoning begins with explanations and examples in early mathematics and develops into formal proof. These domains give that work explicit learning objectives while supporting reasoning throughout all other domains.

| Domain | Intended topics | Connections to further study |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Logic, sets, and proof | Statements, logical connectives, quantifiers, sets, relations, equivalence relations, definitions, examples and counterexamples, direct proof, contrapositive, contradiction, and mathematical induction | Supports rigorous work in discrete mathematics, analysis, abstract algebra, and topology. |
| Discrete mathematics and combinatorics | Counting principles, permutations, combinations, binomial coefficients, pigeonhole arguments, inclusion-exclusion, recurrences, graph theory, trees, and introductory algorithmic reasoning | Supports probability, computing, network problems, and optimization. |
| Number theory | Divisibility, primes, greatest common divisors, least common multiples, Euclid's algorithm, modular arithmetic, congruences, elementary Diophantine equations, and introductory arithmetic functions | Extends arithmetic into proof and structure; connects to abstract algebra and mathematical aspects of cryptography. |

## University mathematics

These domains build on overlapping sets of earlier skills. They are separate entry points for browsing and course mappings, not a single mandatory learning sequence.

| Domain | Intended topics | Important connections |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Linear algebra | Vectors, matrices, linear systems, vector spaces, subspaces, span, linear independence, bases, dimension, linear maps, determinants, eigenvalues and eigenvectors, inner products, orthogonality, and least squares | Connects algebra and geometry to multivariable calculus, statistics, differential equations, numerical methods, and optimization. |
| Calculus | Limits, continuity, derivatives, integrals, the fundamental theorem of calculus, applications, sequences and series, power series, parametric and polar curves, partial derivatives, multiple integrals, and vector calculus | Describes change and accumulation; connects functions and geometry to analysis and mathematical modeling. |
| Differential equations and dynamical systems | First-order ordinary differential equations, initial-value problems, higher-order linear equations, systems, qualitative behavior, equilibria, stability, introductory partial differential equations, and boundary-value problems | Uses calculus and linear algebra; connects physical and biological models to numerical methods. |
| Numerical mathematics | Floating-point arithmetic, approximation error, conditioning, convergence, root finding, interpolation, numerical differentiation and integration, numerical linear algebra, and numerical solutions of differential equations | Connects exact mathematical models to reliable computation and error interpretation. |
| Optimization | Objectives and constraints, linear programming, convexity, unconstrained and constrained optimization, gradient methods, and introductory discrete optimization | Draws on algebra, calculus, linear algebra, and discrete mathematics; supports allocation, fitting, and decision problems. |
| Real and complex analysis | Real-number completeness, rigorous limits and continuity, sequences and series of functions, differentiation and integration theory, introductory metric spaces, complex functions, analyticity, contour integration, and residues | Deepens calculus through definitions and proof; connects to topology and differential equations. |
| Abstract algebra | Groups, subgroups, homomorphisms, quotient structures, rings, ideals, fields, polynomial structures, and introductory field extensions | Develops structure from arithmetic, algebra, symmetry, and proof; connects to number theory and linear algebra. |
| Topology and further geometry | Open and closed sets, continuity between spaces, compactness, connectedness, introductory topological spaces, and introductory curves and surfaces | Develops geometric and analytical reasoning using proof, analysis, and linear algebra. |

## Shared skills and applications

Domain boundaries must not duplicate knowledge. For example, combinations can be practiced in discrete mathematics and used in probability; least squares can appear in linear algebra and statistics; complex arithmetic can support algebra, trigonometry, and complex analysis. Reference the same skill wherever the learning objective is the same. Create separate skills when the mathematical capability genuinely differs, such as calculating a derivative and proving a differentiability claim.

Modeling, problem solving, mathematical communication, estimation, visualization, and computational exploration run across domains. They should appear in learning objectives and exercise activities throughout the content, rather than become a final level unlocked after procedural practice.

Application paths may use personal finance, science, engineering, computing, or everyday data. A finance context can combine percentages, exponential functions, and statistics without creating finance-specific copies of those skills. Context-specific assumptions and interpretation can still be explicit learning objectives.

## Progression examples

These examples illustrate useful connections. They are not complete prerequisite specifications or rules requiring every learner to follow the same route.

| Learning goal | Example supporting skills |
| --- | --- |
| Solve a two-step linear equation | Equality, inverse operations, signed arithmetic, and substitution to check a solution |
| Differentiate a polynomial | Function notation, powers, algebraic manipulation, and understanding a derivative as a rate of change |
| Solve a linear system with matrices | Linear equations, vectors, matrix operations, and equivalence-preserving row operations |
| Interpret a confidence interval | Sampling variation, relevant probability distributions, estimation, and the assumptions of the chosen method |
| Prove convergence of a sequence | Quantifiers, inequalities, real-number properties, and the definition of convergence |

A university learner may need to revisit a foundational skill. The profile should retain evidence for each skill across course changes and prerequisite practice, without treating an educational stage as the learner's overall ability level.

## Building coverage incrementally

Begin with the chosen learner need and a small connected set of skills. For each topic introduced:

1. State observable learning objectives and reuse existing skills where appropriate.
2. Identify and review the relevant prerequisites, including those in other domains.
3. Provide suitable exercise families, explanations, and representations with accessible operation and localization boundaries.
4. Declare what each response demonstrates and how it is validated. Keep mathematical mistakes, invalid input, partial results, and responses needing review distinct.
5. Test generated mathematics and reproducibility, then add curriculum or course mappings where useful.

University coverage includes proof and open-ended reasoning. Such exercises can use structured criteria or educator review where reliable automatic validation is unavailable. Adding a domain does not establish a general symbolic-equivalence engine or automatic proof grader.

Coverage depth, canonical skill IDs, course mappings, and delivery order will be refined through actual content development. No domain is considered covered merely because it has a name in this map or one illustrative exercise.
